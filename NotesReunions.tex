Afterwards, we listed some avenues for the state of the art. We will start by looking for articles on partial preferences focus on indivisible resources.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \b{Acceptability:} Some researchers work on partial preferences under questions of acceptability by considering graphs (bipartite). For example, in the marriage problem, some agents refuse an allowance altogether. Thus, not all objects are allocable to a given agent. Acceptability therefore amounts to defining a list of preferences by excluding those on which the agent issues a right of veto.
    \item \b{Additivity:} Combining individual preferences into bundles of preferences adds information and complexity.
    \item \b{Metric distortion problem:} Ordinal preferences can be deduced from cardinal preferences and used as partial preferences. We then evaluate the extent to which the solution is degraded compared to what could have been proposed with the initial cardinal preferences.
    \item \b{Cardinal preferences:}	Agents can express their preference in cardinal form, i.e. the intensity of the benefit they will derive from the allocation. Although this is suitable for evaluating equity criteria (maxmin, envy-free allocation), it is often difficult for agents to express such preferences.
    \item \b{Truncated ballots:} Derive from the vote theory.
    \item \b{Uncertain preferences:} The preferences are described by a probability distribution.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Topics Discussed}

To prepare for the meeting, I conducted a brainstorming session to identify key concepts, reflections, and questions arising from my recent readings and from previous discussions. The following points were noted:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Agents can be partially known.
    \item Some voting rules cannot be defined solely as a function of a voter's ranking. This is the case for approval voting and range voting, which require additional information beyond a strict ordinal ranking.
    \item Mallows Ranking Models: probabilistic frameworks for modeling and analyzing rankings.
    \item Complete preference information is not always necessary to reach a decision. A key question is determining how much information, or which structural properties, are sufficient to conclude. This motivates the study of \textit{necessary} and \textit{possible} outcomes. Instead of considering all allocations, one can restrict attention to possible allocations, and further to necessary allocations, thereby reducing the search space by excluding impossible solutions.
    \item It is sometimes possible to translate preferences from one representation to another. For example, ordinal preferences can be derived from cardinal preferences, although this transformation results with a loss of information.
    \item The notion of an interval representation requires clarification. In particular, it is important to understand how intervals relate to linear orders, and whether an interval representation such as $[a,b,c]$ can be interpreted as a weak order (e.g., $a \succeq b \succeq c$) or whether it conveys fundamentally different information.
\end{itemize}

I then continued the state-of-the-art review by skimming the article by Kenig and Kimelfeld (2019), as well as the article "Representing Preferences Using Intervals" by Öztürk, Pirlot, and Tsoukiàs (2011).

The first article studies positional scoring rules under probabilistic voting models, in particular when preferences are incomplete. The authors consider two probabilistic models: a uniform distribution over all completions of a partial voting profile, and the Repeated Insertion Model (RIM). Each voter is associated with a scoring vector that assigns points to candidates depending on their position in the ranking.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Plurality rule:} the vector contains a 1 for the most preferred alternative and 0 for all others.
    \item \textbf{Borda rule:} the vector contains $m-1$ points for the most preferred alternative, $m-2$ for the second most preferred alternative, and so on down to 0 for the least preferred alternative. Candidate receives points depending on its rank in each voter's preference list, and the candidate with the highest total score wins.
    \item \textbf{k-approval rule:} the vector contains $k$ entries equal to 1 for the top $k$ the approved alternatives and $m-k$ entries equal to 0 for the disapproved alternatives.
\end{itemize}

Using these scoring vectors, the authors analyze the computational complexity of estimating the probability of an election outcome under uncertainty. Their analysis relies on structural properties of voting rules, in particular the notions of \textit{pure} and \textit{almost constant} scoring rules.

A voting rule is \textit{pure} if the scoring vector for $m$ candidates can be obtained from the scoring vector for $m-1$ candidates by inserting a score for the new candidate without affecting the score of the other candidates. All the previously mentioned rules satisfy this property.

A voting rule is \textit{almost constant} if its scoring vector contains $m-K$ identical scores for some fixed constant $K$. In other words, only a fixed number of candidates receive distinct scores, while the remaining candidates share the same score. The Borda rule does not satisfy this property.

An open question discussed during the meeting was whether the notion of almost constant scoring rules can be extended to preference representations based on intervals.

More broadly, the article expresses the incompleteness/partial preferences and the probability as subfields of the uncertainty.

The second article provided a different perspective on preference representation using semiorders (i.e interval orders). Several observations were noted:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Luce's cup of coffee:} small differences in utility may not be distinguishable, even though larger differences are meaningful. This motivates interval-based representations of preferences. More illustratively, there is no difference between a little more sugar and no sugar at all. However, it is preferable to have more sugar than not at all to soften the bitter taste of coffee.
    \item \textbf{Transitivity:} indifference is not necessarily transitive in semiorders. An agent may be indifferent between $a$ and $b$, and between $b$ and $c$, without being indifferent between $a$ and $c$. This is an important consideration when working with interval representations.
    \item \textbf{Fishburn's classification:} Fishburn identifies nine types of semiorders, providing a theoretical framework for studying such representations.
\end{itemize}
However, during the meeting, we decided not to include this article in the state of the art, as its definition of intervals differs from the one relevant to our project. In our work, intervals are considered in the context of responsive set extensions.

To illustrate the concept of responsive set extension, we consider a set of objects with cardinal utilities:

\begin{tabular}{|p{5cm}|p{2cm}|p{2cm}|p{2cm}|p{2cm}|}
    \hline
    objects & $o_1$ & $o_2$ & $o_3$ & $o_4$ \\
    \hline
    cardinal preferences & 10 & 9 & 8 & 7 \\
    \hline
    strict ordinal preference & \multicolumn{4}{|c|}{ $o_1 \succ o_2 \succ o_3 \succ o_4$ } \\
\end{tabular}

From this ranking, we can conclude, for example, that: $\{o_1, o_2\} \succ \{o_3,o_4\}$

However, some bundle comparisons remain undetermined, such as between $\{o_1, o_4\}$ and other bundles.

The objective is to give a formal interpretation to the following representation:

\begin{tabular}{ |p{5cm}|p{2cm}|p{2cm}|p{2cm}|p{2cm}|  }
    \hline
    objects & $o_1$ & $o_2$ & $o_3$ & $o_4$ \\
    \hline
    intervals & $[5,8]$ & $[6,7]$ & $[4,7]$ & $[8, 10]$ \\
    \hline
    deduced ordinal preferences & 5 & 7 & 6 & 8 \\
     \hline
\end{tabular}

Using these intervals, the value of bundles may be obtained by summing interval bounds. For example:
\\ $v(\{o_1, o_2\}) \in [5+6, 8+7] = [11, 15]$
\\ $v(\{o_1, o_3\}) \in [5+4, 8+7] = [9, 15] $
\\ $v(\{o_3, o_4\}) \in [4+8, 7+10] = [12, 17] $

However, not all values within an interval are equally plausible, as some combinations of individual utilities may be more likely than others. For instance, the values 6+6 = 12 and 5+7 = 12 for the bundle $\{o_1, o_2\}$.

From this representation, the evaluation of possible/necessary envy-freeness or other properties is enabled.

``Computational Complexity of Necessary Envy-Freeness'' by Aziz et al. (2024).

Before discussing the article in detail, we reviewed the main research questions that have already been addressed in the literature and that are related to our topic.
\paragraph{Existence and complexity of envy-freeness.}
The problem of checking whether there exists a complete necessary envy-free (NEF) allocation is NP-complete in general, and polynomial-time solvable when there are only two agents. 
In contrast, checking the existence of a complete possible envy-free (PEF) allocation is computationally tractable.
Related work also studies envy-freeness for bundles induced by ordinal preferences ("Fair Division under Ordinal Preferences: Computing Envy-Free Allocations of Indivisible Goods" by Bouveret et al.).

\paragraph{Possible and necessary winner problems.}
Given a partial voting profile and a positional scoring rule:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The \textit{possible winner problem} asks whether a given candidate can win in at least one completion of the profile. This problem is NP-complete in general, but polynomial-time solvable for specific rules such as plurality and veto.
    \item The \textit{necessary winner problem} asks whether a candidate wins in every completion of the partial profile. This problem is polynomial-time solvable for all positional scoring rules.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Winning probability problem.}
Another related line of work studies the computational complexity of estimating the probability of an election outcome under uncertainty (e.g., Kenig and Kimelfeld, 2019).

\paragraph{Inference rules for bundle preferences.}
Several works analyze how preferences over bundles can be inferred from preferences over single goods. In particular, inference rules based on linear orders over single goods have been studied (e.g., Brams and King; Brams et al.).

At this stage, we have sufficient material to write a structured state of the art. The focus of the meeting shifted toward identifying a clear research question.

\subsection*{Discussion on Aziz et al. (2024)}

During the meeting, we discussed the approach taken by Aziz et al. to describe the envy freeness properties. 
More precisely, an agent is said to prefer one bundle to another if each item in the second bundle can be matched to a distinct item in the first bundle that the agent strictly prefers. 
In other words, the agent can pair every item in the less preferred bundle with a strictly better item in the other bundle.

The extension of preferences induced partial preferences, which should be included in our state of the art.

\subsection*{Clones and Structured Preferences}

We then discussed the notion of \textit{clones}, as mentioned in the survey by Jérôme Lang.
Two alternatives are considered clones if they always appear consecutively in preference rankings (although possibly in different internal orders across agents).
Importantly, no external alternative can appear between them.
\\For instance, in a political election context, two ideologically close candidates may behave as clones: they always appear consecutively in voters’ rankings, although their internal order may vary across voters.

The notion of clones is particularly interesting for our project because it suggests a structural form of indeterminacy that is conceptually close to interval-based representations.

This led to the following perspective:

Suppose that objects can be partitioned into categories (or groups), where objects within the same group are substitutes. 
For example, train and plane tickets to the same destination may be substitutable, whereas a train ticket and a hotel booking may be complementary. If we allocate together the train ticket and the hotel booking, the bundle will have more impact than if we allocate the train ticket with the plane ticket.

Hence, we consider the following structured model:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Agents have strict linear preferences over single objects.
    \item The set of objects is partitioned into $k$ groups $M_1, \dots, M_k$ with $k \ge 1$.
    \item Objects within each group $M_j$ are clones: they appear consecutively in each agent's ranking, though possibly in different internal orders.
    \item The ranking between groups is known.
    \item The internal ranking within each group is unknown.
\end{itemize}

This framework can be interpreted as a structured form of partial preferences.
It generalizes the baseline case (ordinal preference profile) in which each group contains exactly one object.

An open question is whether this model corresponds to an already studied form of partial preference representation.

\paragraph{Resulting questions.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Under clone-group structured partial preferences, what is the complexity of checking existence of NEF allocation?
    \item Does clone-group structure make the NEF problem tractable?
    \item Does clone-group uncertainty behave similarly to matching uncertainty?
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Connections with Matching Theory}

We briefly discussed potential links with matching theory.

\paragraph{Stable matching.}
In the stable marriage setting, instability arises when a student and a university prefer each other to their assigned partners.

\paragraph{Popular matching.} 
A matching A is popular if there is not exist another matching A' such as the number of agents who prefer the matching A' is superior to the number of agents who prefer the matching A.
A popular matching does not always exist, and its existence must be characterized.

The matching is a bijection between the set of agents and the set of objects. In our model, the set of object is divisible into |N| groups (a group can be empty).

Matching problems may provide useful tools for analyzing allocations under structured partial preferences.
